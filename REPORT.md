# Kalman Filter Based Distance and Relative Speed Estimation for Autonomous Vehicle Safety

## Introduction

**Implementation update:** Two new closed-loop road demonstrations, `following` and `obstacle`, now supplement the original five experiments below. They use world-coordinate animation, a single in-lane target, four stationary roadside objects with noisy range observations, and simple estimated-state braking. Lane membership and object identities are given by the simulator. The road KF additionally uses known ego acceleration as input `G*(-a_ego)`; it does not observe target truth. The following lead brakes from 18 to 6 m/s; in the other scenario the target is stationary at 100 m.

Across seeds 42–61, mean distance RMSE is 2.007 m raw versus 1.206 m KF for following, and 2.007 m raw versus 0.697 m KF for the obstacle. Corresponding KF speed RMSE values are 1.733 and 2.041 m/s, compared with approximately 28.7 m/s from raw range differences. All 40 default road trials maintained positive true gaps, and all obstacle trials stopped ego. These finite simulated results do not establish safety outside the tested settings. Comparisons use one driven trajectory per trial, not separate controllers with and without KF.

Reproduce using `python main.py --demo --headless --trials 20 --output outputs/road_trials`. Road motion, executed speeds, braking commands, and roadside ranges are saved to `road_sensor.csv`. Seven automated tests now include control-input prediction and the two road scenarios across 20 seeds. The remaining sections describe the original five open-loop estimation experiments; their historical results remain unchanged.

The aim is to estimate the longitudinal gap and relative speed between an ego vehicle and a lead vehicle using noisy range observations. Filtering can reduce measurement noise and provide relative-speed estimates without directly measuring speed. This simulation evaluates a linear Kalman filter under cruising, approach, braking, increased sensor noise, and temporary measurement loss.

## Methodology

The state is `[distance, lead_speed - ego_speed]ᵀ`. A negative relative speed indicates closing. The transition matrix is `[[1, dt], [0, 1]]`, and the observation matrix is `[[1, 0]]`. Unobserved relative acceleration is represented by process covariance `Q = sigma_a² G Gᵀ`, where `G = [dt²/2, dt]ᵀ`. Measurement variance is `R = sigma_z²`. See README.md for the full prediction, correction, initialization, and Joseph covariance equations.

In the original five experiments, the filter sees only range measurements, never ground-truth states. Missing observations cause prediction-only steps. Time-to-collision is calculated from the estimated states after correction. Illustrative caution and warning thresholds are five and three seconds. These original experiments have no automatic braking controller; the new road demonstrations add the controller described above.

## Implementation

The implementation uses NumPy for matrix operations, Matplotlib for plots and animation, and Pillow for optional GIF export. The filter itself is implemented directly rather than delegated to a filtering library. All scenarios use scripted vehicle motion with Gaussian sensor noise generated by reproducible seeds. Default settings are 25 seconds, a 0.1-second sample interval, 2 m sensor standard deviation, and 2 m/s² discrete process acceleration standard deviation. The noisy scenario uses a 6 m sensor standard deviation and a matching R.

## Discussion

At constant relative speed, the model fits the simulated motion well and the filter reduces range noise. The finite-difference speed baseline amplifies independent range noise: its noise standard deviation is approximately `sqrt(2) sigma_z / dt`, or 28.3 m/s at the default settings. The comparison demonstrates the benefit of temporal modelling, but this is a simple unfiltered baseline, not a claim of superiority over all alternative estimators.

During braking, the constant-velocity prediction lags behind actual speed changes. Range and speed errors increase, and the nominal 95% uncertainty interval covers substantially fewer than 95% of true distances. This indicates model mismatch and imperfect uncertainty calibration. Raising process uncertainty can improve responsiveness but may increase noise; tuning should be assessed across scenarios.

The dropout deliberately overlaps an evolving braking manoeuvre. Prediction continues, covariance grows, and the state drifts because observations are unavailable. Full-run range error can exceed the available-sample raw sensor error. This is a meaningful limitation, and the dropout should not be presented as guaranteed noise reduction.

TTC assumes current relative speed persists. It is sensitive to estimated speed near zero and does not account for future driver response, road friction, vehicle dimensions beyond the defined bumper gap, or braking capability. Warnings are demonstrations, not validated safety decisions.

## Verification

Five automated tests check exact constant-speed convergence, prediction-only dropout propagation, reproducibility and valid covariance across all scenarios, TTC sign/closed-gap behaviour, and invalid parameter rejection. They pass with `python -m unittest -v`. Headless plotting and a GIF export were also exercised. A GUI render smoke check verifies that graphical figures and animation frames can draw in the installed Matplotlib backend.

## Conclusion

A two-state linear KF provides a compact and explainable solution for longitudinal distance and relative-speed estimation. It reduces noise in the simulated measurement stream while exposing the expected tradeoff between smoothing and manoeuvre responsiveness. Sensor outages during braking and uncertainty undercoverage motivate further work on acceleration models, tuning, and validation using real sensor recordings.
